# SignBuddy — Demo Script

**Team:** Dice Developers · **Runtime:** ~4 minutes + questions
**Slides:** 7 · The same script is in the PowerPoint speaker notes.

---

## Before you start — 2 minute checklist

| Check | Command / action |
|---|---|
| Server running | `npm run dev` → http://localhost:3000 |
| Gemini auth alive | `curl -s localhost:3000/api/health` returns `ok` |
| Camera permission granted | Open the app once, allow the webcam |
| Volume up | The voice is a feature — they need to hear it |
| Good light on your hands | Backlight is the #1 failure mode |
| Browser zoom at 100% | Layout is tuned for it |
| Pre-load the tab | Don't cold-start in front of judges |

**Have a fallback.** If the venue Wi-Fi dies, the Gemini calls fail. Keep slide 5
(The Product) open — it's real screenshots, so you can talk through it without
touching the network.

## Slide 2 — The Idea (~45s)

> "Every sign-language app on the market today is a flashcard app. They test
> whether you **recognise** a sign. None of them test whether you can actually
> **produce** one.
>
> That's the gap. You can finish an entire course and still not be understood,
> because nobody ever told you your ring finger was in the wrong place.
>
> And the hard part isn't 'right or wrong' — it's being **specific**. So we send
> Gemini the camera frame *and* 21 tracked landmarks per hand, and we ask it for
> the **indices** of the joints that are wrong. Then we paint those joints red on
> the learner's own hand.
>
> That's the whole product in one line: it doesn't score you, it corrects you."

**Key phrase to land:** *it doesn't score you, it corrects you.*

---

## Slide 3 — User Flow (~40s)

> "Five steps, about six seconds a loop.
>
> The step I'd point at is **step three**. Capture is triggered by your hand
> going *still* — not by a click.
>
> That sounds like a small UX nicety. It isn't. If you make the learner reach for
> a mouse, they break the handshape to do it — so you photograph a broken version
> of the exact thing you're trying to assess.
>
> Hands-free capture is the difference between grading the sign and grading the
> reach for the button."

**Key phrase:** *grading the sign, not the reach for the button.*

---

## Slide 4 — Demo / Technical (~70s) ⭐ spend your time here

> "Three Gemini calls, each doing something different.
>
> **One — grading.** This is the interesting one. We don't just send a photo. We
> send the photo **and** 21 landmarks per hand, and we use a response schema to
> force back arrays of *wrong joint indices*. The model isn't writing prose we
> then parse — it's driving the overlay directly. That's why we can turn one
> finger red.
>
> **Two — voice.** Gemini TTS. It returns raw PCM, so we wrap it to WAV
> server-side. We steer it with director's notes rather than SSML, pinned to
> about 140 words a minute.
>
> **Three — Any Sign**, and this one's our favourite. Gemini returns an ordered
> list of *block names*. It chooses its own layout, we just render it.
> Agent-to-UI."

**Point at the image:** left is live two-hand tracking, right is Gemini's verdict
painted back onto the skeleton.

### If asked "what was hard?" — the 191KB story

> "Our first version let the model emit free-form UI components. It collapsed —
> looped a single malformed block until it produced **191 kilobytes** of
> unparseable JSON.
>
> The fix was to invert it. The model still owns the **layout** — which blocks
> appear and in what order — but the content comes back in typed fields that we
> compose. Clean every time since.
>
> Same class of bug elsewhere: we sort detected hands left-to-right rather than
> trusting MediaPipe's detection order, because it can swap them between frames —
> and then the red joints land on the wrong hand."

## Slide 5 — The Product (~45s) ⭐ go live here if you can

**If the demo gods are kind, drive the actual app instead of the slide.**

> "Left: your coach. The mascot reacts to how you did, speaks the verdict, and
> holds up the shape you're aiming for. The mascot itself can be generated by
> Gemini.
>
> Middle: the reference chart. We cut all 52 letters out of real BSL and ASL
> fingerspelling charts — segmented programmatically, background removed, centred.
>
> Right: the quiz. And this is the one to land. Five prompts. You get the **name**
> of the sign and nothing else. No picture, no steps, no diagram. Then a score out
> of five, and you only see the reference images in the review at the end.
>
> Flashcard apps can test whether you recognise a sign. This tests whether you can
> produce one. That's a different product."

### Live demo running order (if going live — keep it to 60s)

1. **Camera Studio** — hold up **A**. Let the 3-2-1 fire on its own. Say nothing
   while it counts; let them notice you didn't touch anything.
2. Let the voice play. Don't talk over it.
3. **Deliberately sign it wrong** (open your fist) — show the red joints. *This is
   the money shot.* Point at the named chips: "index tip, thumb tip".
4. Switch **ASL → BSL**, hold up both hands, show two skeletons in different colours.
5. **Quiz Challenge** → Start → show the prompt is name-only. Don't finish all 5.

**If a call fails live:** "That's the venue Wi-Fi, not the app — here's the same
flow on the slide." Move on immediately. Do not debug on stage.

## Q&A — prepared answers

**"How accurate is it?"**
> Gemini reads handshape well, and we lean on the landmarks for the joint-level
> call. Lighting is the main failure mode, and we're deliberately lenient there —
> we'd rather pass a correct sign in bad light than frustrate a beginner.

**"Why not train your own classifier?"**
> 26 letters across two systems needs a labelled dataset we don't have in a
> hackathon. Landmarks plus a vision model gets us *per-joint* feedback with no
> training data at all — and a classifier would only ever give us a label, not
> "your ring finger is wrong."

**"Does it work for real signing, not just the alphabet?"**
> Not yet, and we'd rather say so. The alphabet is static; real BSL is motion and
> facial grammar. Landmarks over time is the obvious next step — we already have
> the per-frame pipeline.

**"Is it accessible to the people it's for?"**
> The feedback is visual first — the red joints work with the sound off. The voice
> is additive, for hearing learners studying to communicate with Deaf friends and
> family, which is most of that 12.7 million.

**"What's the latency?"**
> Tracking is local and realtime. The Gemini grading round-trip is a couple of
> seconds, which is why the countdown exists — it gives you something to watch.

**"Does it need the internet?"**
> Tracking doesn't — the wasm and hand model are self-hosted, no CDN. Only the
> Gemini calls need network.
